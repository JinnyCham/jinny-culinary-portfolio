My Story

A baker who
grew into kitchens

Croissants, egg-washed before baking — Universal Bakehouse, 2024

I baked my first bread at 15. Watching the dough rise, learning to read it, and pulling a loaf out of the oven for the first time — that feeling stuck with me. Bread became the beginning of everything.

Later came pastry, and with it a new kind of challenge. Precision. Ratios. Learning that a beautiful tart is built on decisions you make before you even pick up a spatula. Pastry pushed me to be exacting, but also gave me room to be creative.

Then professional kitchens. The real ones — where consistency matters more than creativity, where you earn respect through repetition, and where pressure either shapes you or breaks you. I found that I wanted to be shaped.

What drives me isn't just technique. It's the moment when someone takes a bite and something shifts in them — when food stops being an object and becomes an experience. That's what I'm always working toward.

Perspective

Learning beyond
the kitchen

I'm drawn to the places where tradition meets a new set of hands. Tokyo showed me this most clearly. Walking into Amam Dacotan or Levain Tokyo, I saw what happens when Japanese precision and aesthetics are applied to European bread — the result isn't imitation. It's something entirely its own.

That tension between what's established and what's possible is where I find myself most curious. In Burgundy, I understood wine regions through the same lens — terroir is just another word for where something comes from shaping what it becomes. I think about that in baking too.

I believe the best food isn't about showing off knowledge. It's about making someone feel at home, or surprised, or moved — even by something as simple as a well-made loaf of bread.

"Tradition and innovation aren't opposites. One gives you roots; the other gives you direction."

— Jinny, on the Tokyo bakery visits
Storytelling

She can also
communicate it

Creating content started as a way to document my own baking experiments. It became something more: a discipline in clarity. When you have to explain why bread needs to rest, or how fermentation works in a sixty-second video, you develop a different kind of understanding.

My channels — @bite_mykitchen_ on Instagram, and Jinny 的发酵笔记 on RedNote — grew organically from genuine sharing. I answer questions in comments, guide viewers through recipes they're trying for the first time, and translate professional techniques into something anyone can follow.

Food communication isn't separate from cooking. For me, it's part of the same impulse: making food feel accessible, warm, and worth paying attention to.
